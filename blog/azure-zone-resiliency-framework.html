Your Architecture Diagram Is Not Your Resilience: A Zone-Resiliency Framework for Azure

Roman Rabodzei · Oct 2026 · ~8 min read
Azure Architecture Resiliency Availability Zones Disaster Recovery Well-Architected

Azure published two related pieces in September 2026 — "Two zones or three? A design framework for zone-resilient Azure workloads" and "Your architecture diagram is not your resilience" — that name a problem I run into constantly during Well-Architected reviews: teams draw a resilient-looking diagram, deploy it, and never actually verify it does what the diagram implies. The diagram shows redundancy. Whether the workload actually survives a zone failure is a separate, unverified claim.

Two zones or three — it's not a rounding decision

Azure regions with availability zones typically offer three zones, and the design framework's honest answer to "two or three?" is: it depends what you're protecting against and what quorum-based systems you're running. Two-zone deployments are cheaper and cover the most common failure mode (losing exactly one zone), but anything relying on quorum — a database cluster, a distributed consensus system — needs three zones to maintain quorum when one zone is lost, because two zones can't out-vote a failed one without an external tiebreaker.

The decision isn't really "two vs three" in the abstract — it's "does any component in this architecture need quorum to make progress?" If yes, three zones aren't a nicety, they're the minimum for the system to keep functioning at all during a zone failure rather than just staying "technically deployed" while actually unavailable.

Where the diagram lies by omission

A diagram showing "App Service, zone redundant" or "AKS cluster, spread across 3 zones" tells you the infrastructure primitive supports zone redundancy — it says nothing about whether your specific configuration actually uses it correctly. The gaps I see most often in review:

  • Zone-redundant services configured, but dependencies aren't. A zone-redundant App Service backed by a single-zone database is exactly as resilient as the database.
  • Health probes that don't actually detect zone-level failure modes. A probe checking "is the process running" won't catch "this zone lost network connectivity to a dependency," which looks identical to the app but fails identically to a full outage from the user's perspective.
  • Autoscaling rules that assume all zones are available. If your scale-out logic doesn't account for losing a third of your capacity instantly, the remaining zones can get overwhelmed by redistributed traffic before they finish scaling up.
  • DNS and traffic manager configuration that isn't actually tested for zone failover — the difference between "configured to failover" and "verified to failover within an acceptable time" is usually a chaos engineering exercise nobody has run yet.

The test that actually matters: can you name the last time you deliberately failed a zone (or simulated it) in a non-production environment and measured what happened, end to end, including dependent services? If the honest answer is "never," the diagram is a design intent, not a verified property of the system.

A practical zone-resiliency framework

  1. Map every component's dependency chain, not just the top-level service — zone redundancy is only as strong as the least-redundant dependency in the chain.
  2. Identify quorum-based components explicitly and confirm they're deployed across three zones, not two, if the region supports it.
  3. Define health checks that reflect real failure modes — dependency reachability, not just process liveness.
  4. Load-test the reduced-capacity scenario — what happens when your autoscaler has to absorb a third of your traffic redistribution instantly, not gradually.
  5. Actually run a zone failure simulation in a non-production environment on a schedule, and track the metrics that matter: time to detect, time to failover, error rate during the transition.

My take

Resilience reviews that stop at "is this marked zone-redundant in the portal" are checking a configuration flag, not a property of the system under failure. The distinction Azure is drawing this quarter — diagram versus verified resilience — matches exactly what I see going wrong in practice: the infrastructure is capable of surviving a zone failure, and nobody has actually confirmed the application and its dependencies are configured to take advantage of that capability end to end.
